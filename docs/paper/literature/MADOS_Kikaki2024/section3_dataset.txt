3.1. The MADOS benchmark dataset 
3.1.1. Data processing & curation 
Satellite sensors with high spatial, spectral and temporal resolution 
are required to develop operational solutions for monitoring the marine 
environment. Currently, the openly available Copernicus S2 multispec -
tral data cover global coastal waters at high spatial resolutions (10 m, 
20 m and 60 m). Additionally, S2 satellite has a frequent revisit time of 
approximately five days (depending on the latitude), delivering a large 
amount of free geospatial data. It also acquires data in 13 spectral bands, 
which range from the visible to the Short-Wave Infrared (SWIR). 
Therefore, in our study, we utilized the S2 multispectral satellite images. 
We collected S2 Level 1C products (Top-of-Atmosphere reflectance) 
through EarthExplorer (USGS). Similar to previous studies on floating 
materials detection ( Qi et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2014 ), 
we applied ACOLITE atmospheric processor ( Vanhellemont and Rud -
dick, 2018 ) to retrieve the Rayleigh reflectance. 
Data annotation, curation and refining were performed following 
Kikaki et al. (2020) & (2022) methods and protocols. The annotation 
was performed by three image-interpretation experts with access to all 
collected data. To enhance the accuracy and reliability of our dataset, 
with a particular focus on Marine Debris and Oil Spill classes, we 
employed an inter-annotators agreement protocol. Specifically, for 
Marine Debris and Oil Spill , the intersection per two annotators was 
extracted (i.e., an agreement between at least two annotators regarding 
the class label). This approach enriched fine-grained annotations of 
Marine Debris , offering greater detail and precision. As Oil Spill was also a 
primary category in our focus on marine pollution, we applied this 
protocol to this class in a similar manner. We also used the same protocol 
to similarly appearing classes to Marine Debris , i.e., Natural Organic 
Material and Sparse Floating Algae . For the other classes, the union of the 
annotated data was calculated. 
To improve the quality of our annotated data, the structure of the 
high-dimensional observations was visualized. Thus, we embedded our 
data in a 2D space, identifying the spectral patterns and outliers. Spe -
cifically, we utilized the t-distributed Stochastic Neighborhood 
Embedding (t-SNE) algorithm ( van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008 ) using 
Spectral Angle Mapping (SAM) as a distance metric. 
Based on the annotated data, we cropped the S2 scenes into non- 
overlapped image patches. In the produced dataset, the initial spatial 
resolution of each spectral band was retained without any upsampling 
technique. Each provided image corresponds to 2.4x2.4 km
2
; thus, the 
10 m spectral bands form image patches of 240x240 pixels, the 20 m 
patches of 120x120 pixels and the 60 m patches of 40x40 pixels. 
3.1.2. MADOS thematic classes and annotations 
We have constructed MADOS, which contains annotated S2 data 
based on 174 scenes and 47 tiles captured between 2015 and 2022. 
Numerous annotations were created consisting of 1,481,155 pixels 
based on studies, reports and data on marine pollution as well as 
intensive photo-interpretation ( Table S1 ). Finally, our dataset is 
distributed over 22 countries ( Fig. 1 ) and contains 15 different thematic 
classes ( Table 3 ).

Regarding Marine Debris , we used the open-access MARIDA dataset 
( Kikaki et al., 2022 ), which distinguishes litter from various sea features. 
We also increased the number of Marine Debris pixels by adding new 
observations based on recent reports and publications ( Table S1 ). 
Following previous studies ( Biermann et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2022; 
Kikaki et al., 2020; Martínez-Vicente et al., 2019 ), we additionally 
considered floating materials that have been suggested to be spectrally 
similar to plastics, or are occasionally aggregated with floating macro -
plastics on the ocean surface. Thus, Foam , Sargassum , Natural Organic 
Material (including driftwood), Ships and Sea Snot trajectories ( Hu et al., 
2022 ) were annotated ( Fig. 2 ). Suspected Jellyfish investigated by Qi 
et al., (2020) were also taken into account to evaluate the discrimination 
from Marine Debris and Sea Snot, as previous studies have indicated the 
potential of spectral similarities ( Hu et al., 2022; Van der Zande et al., 
2014 ). Classes sometimes observed concurrently with oil slicks, such as 
Oil Platform and Sparse Floating Algae, were also considered and added. 
Moreover, sea state look-alikes were annotated and aggregated with the 
Marine Water class. Other water-related classes recorded on our study 
regions (i.e., Sediment-Laden Water, Turbid Water, Shallow Water, Waves 
and Ship Wakes) have also been annotated. Also, sparse Sargassum was 
grouped with other sparse floating algae species, forming a Sparse 
Floating Algae (SpFa) super-class. By adding these competing cases, we 
aim to enable the developed algorithms to discriminate marine pollut -
ants from the other floating materials and sea surface features. We note 
that clouds and cloud shadows were not included in our analysis. We 
consider this a different, quite challenging task that the remote sensing 
scientific community is working on. Therefore, one could integrate any 
of the standard software tools (such as MAJA, Fmask, Sen2Cor) into the 
processing pipeline to address cloud and shadow detection.
